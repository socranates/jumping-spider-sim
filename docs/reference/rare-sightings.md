# Rare Sightings

Thirteen creatures that appear once, or almost never, in the preserve. Each is a moment to remember rather than a meal. The drawings are rough concept sketches for the shapes and colours, not final models.

**Once per life, at most**Each has a trigger: a season, an hour, the weather, a place. Most lives see one or two.

**A life list**The notebook keeps a Rare page per creature, kept across generations, so a family of spiders slowly fills it.

**Watch, don't always hunt**Some can be eaten, some will eat you, and some are simply too big. The game never says which.

**Honest about place**The preserve is in the American Midwest. Creatures from elsewhere are marked, with a reason they could be here.

wingspan 8–11 cm · about 10× you

## Luna moth

Actias luna

nativenightlate springharmless

The sighting

On a warm spring night it comes to the hut's porch lamp and rests by the window until dawn, its long tails turning in the air. It never moves when the spider comes close.

What you can do

Climb onto a wing if you dare, then watch it leave at first light. It is far too big to catch.

Notebook: the adult has no working mouth and lives about a week. Its tails spin as it flies, which seems to fool the sonar of hunting bats.

wingspan to 25 cm · about 25× you

## Atlas moth

Attacus atlas

not native: escapedonce per gameharmless

The sighting

It lives in the forests of Southeast Asia, so here it is a single escapee from the city's conservatory butterfly house, resting on the hut's wall one summer morning. It is the largest thing with wings the spider will ever see up close.

What you can do

Walk its wing from tip to tip. Up close the folded tips look like two snake heads.

Notebook: it never eats as an adult and lives on the fat it stored as a caterpillar. It will not find a mate here. A quiet, lonely page.

3–4 cm · about 4× you

## Wheel bug

Arilus cristatus

nativelate summergoldenroddeadly

The sighting

An assassin bug with a cogwheel on its back sits motionless in the goldenrod. It moves slowly and deliberately, its beak folded under its head.

What you can do

Leave. If it reaches you, its beak goes in and it is over. Seen from a safe distance, it gets a page.

Notebook: assassin bugs inject saliva that turns their prey's insides to liquid, then drink it. Spiders do something very like this too. The two of you hunt the same way.

to 9.5 cm · about 9× you

## Walkingstick

Diapheromera femorata

nativewoodland edgeharmless

The sighting

A twig on the oak sapling sways when there is no wind. The spider can walk along it, and halfway the twig starts walking too.

What you can do

Ride it a few steps to a new branch, or drop off on your line. Too big and too tough to eat.

Notebook: it sways side to side like a twig in a breeze, even indoors. For something that looks like a stick, looking like a stick in the wind is the next step.

5–10 cm adult · 1 cm hatchling

## Praying mantis

Stagmomantis carolina

nativespring hatchdeadly when grown

The sighting

Twice in a year. In spring, a foam egg case on the fence splits and dozens of tiny mantises pour out, small enough for the spider to hunt. In autumn one of them is back, grown, and it turns its head to watch the spider.

What you can do

In spring, eat. In autumn, run. The same animal is prey one season and a predator the next.

Notebook: jumping spiders and mantises eat each other. Which one eats the other depends only on who is bigger at the time.

1.5–2.2 cm · about 2× you

## Rainbow scarab

Phanaeus vindex

nativesummerafter the dogharmless

The sighting

The morning after the roaming dog has passed, a metal-green and copper beetle flies in, finds what the dog left, and digs it into the ground under the path.

What you can do

Watch it dig. Its shell is too hard to bite. The male's single horn curves back over his head.

Notebook: dung beetles bury dung as food for their young. Without them, a field fills with what animals leave behind. Small lives keep the preserve clean.

1–2 cm · about your size

## Grass spider

Agelenopsis sp.

nativelate summer dewdangerous on its web

The sighting

On a dewy morning the meadow's edge shows a sheet of silk with a funnel at one end. Something waits in the funnel.

What you can do

Never step on the sheet. On its web it is faster than you. Off it, you are evenly matched.

Notebook: this is the American funnel weaver. The famous deadly funnel-web spiders live in Australia and are not related closely. A web-builder and a jumper are cousins who solved hunting in opposite ways.

2–3 cm · about 3× you

## Trapdoor spider

Ummidia sp.

native, uncommon this far northbare bankdeadly at the door

The sighting

On the railway bank there is a perfect circle in the soil you never noticed. One evening it lifts a few millimetres, and two dark legs rest at its edge.

What you can do

Walk wide. Silk lines run out from the door, and touching one is enough. If you see the door, you have already found something most people never will.

Notebook: the door is soil and silk on a silk hinge, so well matched to the ground that it disappears when closed. The spider may use the same burrow for years.

4–6 cm · about 6× you

## Hercules beetle

Dynastes tityus

nativesummer nightwoodlandharmless

The sighting

A huge beetle with two horns lands with a thud under the porch lamp. Its wing cases are pale olive with dark spots, and no two have the same pattern.

What you can do

Climb it like a hill. It barely notices you. Males use the horns to pry rivals off branches.

Notebook: one of the heaviest beetles in North America. Its grub spends a year or two in rotting wood before it becomes this.

about 1 cm · your size

## Bombardier beetle

Brachinus sp.

nativedamp ground at nighta lesson

The sighting

A small orange and blue beetle under a stone looks like an easy meal. It is the only creature here that answers with a pop.

What you can do

Try to eat it once. It fires a boiling spray from its tail, the spider lets go, and you go hungry. The notebook page comes from the mistake.

Notebook: two chemicals mix in a chamber in its abdomen and react hot enough to boil, in rapid pulses. Even toads that swallow one have been seen to spit it back out alive.

about 2 cm · 2× you

## Velvet ant

Dasymutilla occidentalis

nativehot sandy pathdo not touch

The sighting

On the hottest afternoon of summer, a fuzzy red and black "ant" runs fast across the gravel. It is a wingless wasp.

What you can do

Nothing, wisely. If grabbed it squeaks and stings, and its body armour is too hard to pierce. Its colours are a warning that tells the truth.

Notebook: its nickname is "cow killer" for a sting that is very painful, though it is not deadly to cows. Only the females are wingless; the males fly.

1–2 cm · about your size

## The false firefly

Photuris sp. hunting Photinus

nativeearly summer duska lie in light

The sighting

For a few June evenings the meadow fills with flashes. Most are ordinary fireflies, males and females answering each other. One answer near the spider comes from a hunter.

What you can do

Fireflies at rest can be caught. Watch long enough to see a male fly to the wrong light, and not come back.

Notebook: some female fireflies copy another species' answering flash to lure its males in and eat them. Scientists call them femmes fatales.

about 3 cm · 3× you

## Periodical cicada

Magicicada sp.

nativeone year in seventeenonce per game

The sighting

One night in late spring, nymphs climb out of the ground everywhere at once, split their skins and leave the empty shells clinging to every stem. By morning the trees are loud with red-eyed adults.

What you can do

Feast. There are far more than any predator can eat, and that is the point. For the spider it is a year of plenty, and it may never come again.

Notebook: they spend 13 or 17 years underground, then moult once more into the air. Every moult the spider makes is a smaller version of that night.
