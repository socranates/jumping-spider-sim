# What the spider-eye picture represents

The player is a *Phidippus audax* prototype. This is a research-informed illustration of visual sampling, not a reconstruction of subjective colour, or a measured *P. audax* visual system.

## Evidence and choices

- **Moving retinas behind fixed lenses.** Land (1969), *Movements of the retinae of jumping spiders*, describes horizontal scanning and slower torsion in *Phidippus* and *Metaphidippus*. The reported scanning periods are roughly 1–2 seconds, torsion 5–15 seconds, with approximately 50 degrees of torsional excursion. The game's 1.6-second sweep and 8-second, ±25-degree torsion illustrate these scales. Actual scans depend on stimulus size and attention; the continuous sinusoid, ±4-degree sweep, fixed separation, softened boomerang geometry and 5×20-degree masks are presentation choices, not fitted recordings. [Paper / DOI](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.51.2.471); [full-text copy](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=60b0c4baa376c804a2ae978992c6bf4f8ff64bda&repid=rep1&type=pdf).
- **Different eye roles.** Jakob et al. (2018), *Lateral eyes direct principal eyes as jumping spiders track objects*, reports narrow movable principal-eye retinas and guidance from the motion-sensitive anterior lateral eyes. The combined image therefore retains a coarse surround and moving areas of fine detail. It does not show eight separate screens, simulate a complete all-round field, or treat a retina as an externally rotating eyeball. The game's existing movement cues and player-directed gaze remain responsible for attention. [Primary research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30253146/).
- **Attention is more than a scanning animation.** Bruce et al. (2021), *Attention and distraction in the modular visual system of a jumping spider*, shows flexible redirection of principal-eye gaze to distractors detected by lateral eyes. This pass does not claim to model that decision process or implement prey-locked eye tracking. [Primary research](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33914032/).
- **Green and UV receptors do not imply human blue/green vision.** DeVoe (1975), *Ultraviolet and green receptors in principal eyes of jumping spiders*, measured *P. regius*, including sensitivity peaks near 370 nm and 532 nm. This is related-species evidence, not a direct measurement of this game's *P. audax*. Lack of a demonstrated dedicated red receptor does not mean every red surface is invisible. [Primary research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2226199/).

## Colour limitation

The former shader used the RGB blue channel as UV. That was an invented correlation: two materials with identical visible RGB can have different UV reflectance. This pass removes it. A green-weighted brightness transform makes green-lit surfaces relatively bright and red/blue relatively dim, with a pale green display tint. The weights and tint are illustrative, not calibrated receptor responses, and do not assert that a spider sees a monochrome green world. The persistent label says **UV not simulated**. Simulating UV requires separately authored, evidence-backed reflectance data and a lighting model; ordinary RGB cannot supply it. Species with other colour mechanisms must not inherit this approximation without review.

## Gameplay and comfort boundaries

The two masks overlap in one view and move the distribution of detail only. They do not move the camera, jump ray, body, or sensing cone, and use no gameplay randomness. The old reach fade still applies when the player looks beyond the principal eyes' allowed scan angle. Sweeps pause with the game and stop at their current pose for Reduced motion; switching to orbit disables the effect. Numerical scan tests sample 30, 60 and 144 fps. Depth-from-defocus, spectral UV assets, neural integration and a biologically complete secondary-eye panorama remain unimplemented.

## T13a additions (Claude, 2026-10-11)

- **The surround is motion-sensitive.** Jumping spiders' secondary eyes are wide-field motion detectors. The anterior lateral eyes, the forward-facing secondary pair, can by themselves trigger turning toward a moving dot and even stalking of a tethered fly (Zurek, Taylor, Evans & Nelson 2010, *J. Exp. Biol.* 213: 2372–2378, [doi:10.1242/jeb.042382](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.042382)). They detect movements smaller than the angle between their receptors (Zurek & Nelson 2012, *Vision Research* 66: 26–30). Jakob et al. (2018, above) show these eyes directing the principal eyes. In the game a thing the sensor's sightline checks found moving gets a pulsing ring and a faint halo in the soft surround, where it is drawn. Inside the sharp retinal fields there is none, because there the principal eyes see form. A still thing gets no ring, and neither does a hidden one. The ring's pulse (3 Hz), size and look are presentation choices; the game does not model the lateral eyes' receptor spacing or thresholds. The cue reuses the sensor's existing motion threshold, and it changes no gameplay: nothing reads it but the picture.
- **Depth from defocus is not built, on purpose.** Nagata et al. (2012, *Science* 335: 469–471, [doi:10.1126/science.1211667](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1211667)) showed that *Hasarius adansoni* judges jump distance from how blurred the image is on one layer of the principal retina. Under green light the spiders jumped accurately; under red light, with no green, they jumped short, as the mechanism predicts. A faithful version would make jumps less accurate in dim or reddish light and at longer range. That changes the jump outcomes the movement gates rest on (M01–M03). A landing marker that only looked unsure while the jump still landed exactly would mislead the player. So this waits for Nathan's decision (DECISIONS EYE-13).
- **Comfort options.** The level horizon keeps the world's up in the spider-eye view, so the picture no longer rolls on walls and undersides. This departs from the spider: its eyes are fixed to its head and turn with its body. The grey picture shows the same green-weighted brightness without the green tint. Both are off by default and labelled as comfort settings.
- **UV stays unsimulated (EYE-14), after a literature search.** What the published evidence gives, around the ~370 nm UV receptor peak:
  - **Clear single window glass** transmits about 85–88 % at 380 nm (ASTM G24, seven lots of single-strength glass). Laminated glass blocks UVA almost entirely. So daylight on a sill behind an ordinary pane still carries UVA.
  - **Green leaves** reflect about 5 % at 350–380 nm against about 13–17 % at 530–550 nm. That is a birch spectrum (Aphalo & Lehto 1997, *Tree Physiology* 17: 125–132, read from the `photobiology` package's `green_leaf.spct`); other studies agree that leaves reflect a few percent in UV.
  - **White titanium-dioxide paint** absorbs strongly below about 400 nm, so a white-painted sill would look dark in UV. That much is qualitative only: no measured percentage at 360–380 nm was found for a real interior paint.
  - For non-metal surfaces, UVA reflectance cannot be predicted from visible reflectance (Turner & Parisi 2018, *Building and Environment* 136: 259–268). This confirms that RGB cannot stand in for UV.
  - **Missing:** a measured UV reflectance of the house fly's body or wings, the prey that matters most. Also missing: *P. audax*'s own markings, varnished wood, terracotta and plaster. No spectral sensitivity has been measured in *P. audax* itself; the 370 / 532 nm peaks are *P. regius* (DeVoe 1975). *P. audax* showed no red-filtering retinal pigment (Zurek et al. 2015, *Current Biology*), so there is no evidence for a red channel.
  - A UV picture with leaves and glass right but the flies guessed would be most wrong exactly where the player looks. It stays off until fly UV reflectance can be sourced or measured. Sources marked here as abstracts or standards were not all read in full; check them before building on them.
